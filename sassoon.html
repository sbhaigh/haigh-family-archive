Eyewitness · 1 July 1916

Sassoon at Fricourt

One of the best-known literary witnesses to the First World War was looking across the same battlefield as Ernest Harmson and the 10th West Yorkshires went forward.

Crawley Ridge

Lieutenant Siegfried Sassoon, 1st Battalion Royal Welch Fusiliers, was in reserve on 1 July. His company had carrying duties, leaving him able to observe the opening attack from Crawley Ridge behind the British line.

Cambridge University Library, which holds his journals, places him about 500 yards behind the front line. His surviving journal contains timed observations, a hand-drawn map and the entries he made as the battle unfolded.

The significance to this family archive is unusually direct: Cambridge identifies the men Sassoon watched advancing to his left at the opening of the battle as the 10th West Yorkshire Regiment — Ernest Harmson's battalion.
Crawley Ridge is marked behind the British line. The dotted sight-line is interpretive; it shows the relationship between Sassoon’s observation area and the West Yorkshire attack rather than a precise field of view.
07:30 · 1 July 1916

An eyewitness to the attack

“cheering as if at a football match”

That brief phrase in Sassoon's journal is the most immediate surviving literary connection to Ernest's battalion as it went forward.

Cambridge's analysis states that the attack Sassoon was describing was made by the 10th West Yorkshires, away to his left. The same account identifies a German machine gun at Wing Corner as causing enormous casualties after the artillery intended to suppress it proved ineffective.

We cannot know whether Sassoon saw Ernest individually. What the evidence does allow us to say is more restrained and more powerful: he watched Ernest's battalion begin the attack in which Ernest was killed.

A diary written in real time

Sassoon recorded observations throughout the morning. At 09:50 he noted men still advancing in small groups to his left; shortly afterwards he recorded the 21st Division continuing across the skyline towards La Boisselle.

His journal for 26 June–12 August survives as Cambridge manuscript MS Add. 9852/1/7. The notebook also contains a plan of the trench lines and cemetery, later accounts of fighting around Mametz Wood, drafts of poetry and other wartime notes.

On 13 July, reflecting on the men lost during the offensive, he wrote of their hope and merriment being extinguished and their voices passing from memory. The archive uses that later reflection sparingly: the contemporary 1 July entries are the primary evidence for what he actually saw at Fricourt.

The map in his notebook

Sassoon did not leave only prose. He sketched the battlefield.

Cambridge describes his folio 2r map as showing the German trenches. The cemetery appears south-west of Fricourt; the British front ran just to its right and continued eastwards. This makes the journal unusually valuable for connecting an eyewitness account to the physical landscape.
